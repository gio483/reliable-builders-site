Allied Gardens is the classic San Diego family neighborhood — a thousand acres of rolling streets in the Navajo area, wedged between Del Cerro and San Carlos, with Mission Trails and the San Diego River valley at its edges. It was built for families from the start, and three generations later that's still exactly who lives here. When homeowners call us for a kitchen, bathroom, or ADU in Allied Gardens, it's usually because the house has been great to them but the original 1950s or '60s layout and systems have finally hit their limit. That's a good problem, and it's the one we solve well.

These are honest, well-made post-war homes. Remodeling them is less about reinventing the house and more about respecting what's there while bringing it current.

## The homes here

Allied Gardens was developed starting in 1955 by a company called the Allied Contractors — that's where the name comes from — on land bought from the old Waring estate, built in part to house returning Allied servicemen and their families after the war. The bulk of the neighborhood went up between the late 1950s and the early 1970s, and the housing stock reflects it: mostly single-story ranch homes, with some split-levels and a thread of Spanish-revival styling mixed in. They tend to be modest, practical, and remarkably consistent block to block.

For a remodel, that consistency actually helps — after 20 years of working on homes of this era, we know what's behind the walls before we open them. In an Allied Gardens kitchen or bath, that usually means original or early-generation supply lines, cast-iron or early-ABS drains, two-prong ungrounded circuits in the older sections, and an electrical panel that was plenty for 1960 and is tight for today. We budget for that reality honestly instead of quoting a thin number that jumps the moment the drywall comes off. It's the difference between a contractor who's done this a hundred times and one who's guessing.

The reward is that these ranch homes are easy to love once they're updated. The galley and U-shaped kitchens of the era open up beautifully into the living space, and a modest post-war floor plan can live far larger than its square footage once the right wall comes down.

## Permitting in Allied Gardens

Allied Gardens is part of the City of San Diego, so permits for remodeling work here run through the **City of San Diego's Development Services Department**. A kitchen or bathroom remodel that stays inside the existing footprint — cabinets, countertops, tile, fixtures, lighting, and like-for-like plumbing and electrical — is a permittable project, and we handle the permits and the inspections so you're never the one standing in line at the City.

The step up in complexity is anything structural or anything that grows the home: taking out a wall to open a kitchen (we confirm whether it's load-bearing and engineer a beam if it is), relocating plumbing, replacing an undersized panel, or adding square footage. The City of San Diego's review is more formal than the smaller East County cities', so we fold the permit timeline into the plan from the beginning. That way the paperwork is moving in the background instead of becoming the thing that stalls your project halfway through.

## ADUs in Allied Gardens

ADUs — accessory dwelling units, or granny flats — fit Allied Gardens especially well, because this is a multigenerational family neighborhood and the most common reasons people build one (space for an aging parent, a landing spot for an adult kid, or rental income) are everywhere here. California's statewide ADU laws have made them far easier to build than a decade ago, and the City of San Diego runs its own program on top of that framework (the local rules live in San Diego Municipal Code Chapter 14), including a streamlined track for qualifying units.

Allied Gardens' generous, relatively flat lots and detached garages make it one of the more ADU-friendly pockets of the Navajo area — garage conversions and detached backyard units both tend to pencil out here. The specifics — how big, how close to the property line, whether your lot and utilities support it — come down to your exact parcel, and that's the first thing we check before anyone commits to a design. We handle the feasibility review, the permit set, the engineering, and the build, so the finished ADU is legal, financeable, and somewhere you'd be glad to put family. Most ADU projects start around **$150K** and rise with size and finishes.
