When you start getting quotes, you'll notice something: the unlicensed crews are almost always cheaper. Sometimes a lot cheaper. It's tempting, especially on a big project where every dollar counts.

So let's be honest about what that lower price really means — what you're saving, and what you might be risking.

## What a contractor's license actually is

In California, a general contractor has to be licensed by the state (the CSLB — Contractors State License Board). To get that license, they have to prove their experience, pass exams, and carry the right paperwork. It's not just a formality. It's the state saying, "This person has shown they know how to do this work and can be held accountable."

You can look up any license yourself on the CSLB website. A legitimate builder gives you the number without hesitating. Ours is **CSLB #1139785**.

## What the license actually buys you

A license isn't a sticker. It comes with real protections for you as the homeowner:

- **Accountability.** If a licensed contractor does bad work or disappears, you have somewhere to turn — the state board. With an unlicensed person, you're mostly on your own.
- **Insurance and bonding.** Licensed contractors carry insurance. If someone gets hurt on your job, or your home gets damaged, that coverage protects you. Without it, the liability can land on *you*.
- **Proper permits.** Licensed builders pull permits and pass inspections, so your work is on record and done to code.
- **A real warranty.** A licensed company that plans to be around next year stands behind its work. A cash crew that's gone tomorrow can't.

That's what the extra cost pays for. It's less "paying more for the same thing" and more "paying for a safety net."

## The real risks of going unlicensed

Here's what can go wrong when you hire an unlicensed crew to save money:

- **No recourse if it goes bad.** If the work is wrong and they walk, your options shrink fast. Chasing someone with no license and no insurance is hard and often fruitless.
- **You may be on the hook for injuries.** If an uninsured worker gets hurt on your property, you could end up responsible for the medical bills. That one risk alone can dwarf anything you saved.
- **Unpermitted work bites later.** Unlicensed crews often skip permits. When you go to sell, buyers and lenders ask for records — and unpermitted work can sink the deal or force an expensive redo.
- **The "cheap" job gets expensive.** A surprising number of our calls are from people who hired cheap, got poor work, and now need a licensed builder to fix it. They end up paying twice.

The lowest bid isn't a deal if it leaves you exposed.

## When cheaper feels fine (and when it really isn't)

For tiny, low-stakes jobs — hanging a shelf, a quick paint touch-up — hiring help isn't a big risk. But the moment the work touches plumbing, electrical, structure, or square footage, the stakes jump. That's exactly the work in a [kitchen](/services/kitchens), a [bathroom](/services/bathrooms), or an [ADU](/services/adus) — and exactly where a license matters most.

A good rule of thumb: the bigger the project and the more it affects the safety and value of your home, the more a license is worth. On a $40K kitchen or a $150K+ ADU, saving a few thousand by going unlicensed is a gamble with long odds.

## How this ties to the pushy-sales problem

There's a flip side worth naming. Being licensed doesn't automatically make a company *pleasant* to work with. Some of San Diego's biggest licensed remodel outfits are known for high-pressure sales — the rep who won't leave your house until you sign, the price that drops if you commit tonight. Licensed, yes. Honest and easy to work with? Not always.

So the goal isn't just "licensed." It's **licensed *and* honest.** You want the paperwork that protects you *and* a builder who educates you, respects your budget, and lets you decide without pressure. You deserve both.

## How to check in two minutes

Before you hire anyone, do this:

1. **Ask for the license number.** A real one gives it freely.
2. **Look it up** on the CSLB website to confirm it's active and in good standing.
3. **Ask for proof of insurance.**
4. **Ask who's really doing the work** and whether they're pulling permits.

Four quick steps. They take a couple minutes and can save you a world of trouble.

## The bottom line

An unlicensed crew will usually quote you less. But that lower number can leave you without accountability, without insurance protection, without permits, and without a warranty. On a real remodel, that's a lot to give up to save a little.
