Fletcher Hills is one of those East County neighborhoods that feels like its own small town — curving streets climbing the hills, big mature trees, and solid homes from the post-war boom with views you don't get down in the valley. We're based just west in La Mesa, so this is familiar ground: the 1950s and '60s hillside houses, the split-levels, and the quirks that come with homes built when the neighborhood was first carved into the slopes.

There's one thing about Fletcher Hills that trips up more than a few homeowners and, honestly, more than a few contractors: the neighborhood sits right on a city line. That has real consequences for how your remodel gets permitted — and it's the kind of detail we sort out before we ever quote a job.

## The homes here

Fletcher Hills traces back to the late 1920s, when San Diego developer Ed Fletcher first laid out the area, but the bulk of what you see today was built out in the post-war surge of the **1950s and '60s**. That's the heart of the neighborhood's housing stock: single-story and split-level ranch homes, mid-century designs stepped onto hillside lots, and a scattering of newer and remodeled houses mixed in.

For a remodel, 1950s–60s hillside construction tells us a lot before we ever walk in. Homes from that era were wired, plumbed, and framed to the codes of their day, so we routinely open up Fletcher Hills kitchens and baths to find things like galvanized supply lines, aging two-prong wiring with no ground, cast-iron drains, and the occasional un-permitted "upgrade" from a past decade. On the hillside lots, the slope adds its own wrinkles — framing and access that only made sense to the original builder. None of that is cause for alarm. It's simply what remodeling a well-built older home involves, and it's the exact reason we give you an honest number up front instead of a lowball that balloons the first time we see the real condition behind the walls.

The payoff is that these homes have good bones, generous lots, and the kind of light and proportion you can build something special around. A Fletcher Hills kitchen or bath done right updates the systems and finishes to today without erasing what made the house worth buying.

## Permitting in Fletcher Hills

This is the part worth reading twice. Fletcher Hills **straddles a city line** — most of the neighborhood sits in the **City of El Cajon**, while its western edge falls inside the **City of La Mesa**, and the broader East County around it includes pockets of unincorporated San Diego County. That means there is no single answer to "who issues my permit" for Fletcher Hills as a whole. **It depends on which side of the line your specific property sits on.**

Why it matters: an El Cajon address goes through El Cajon's building department and its permitting system, while a La Mesa parcel goes through the City of La Mesa, and a truly unincorporated parcel would go through the County. Each has its own process, its own fees, and its own review timelines. A contractor who assumes the wrong one can waste weeks. So the very first thing we do on any Fletcher Hills project is confirm the exact jurisdiction for your address, then pull the permits and schedule inspections through the right office — El Cajon, La Mesa, or County — so you never have to untangle it yourself.

For most kitchen and bath remodels that stay inside the existing footprint — cabinets, counters, tile, fixtures, lighting, and like-for-like plumbing and electrical — that's a standard building permit wherever your parcel lands. Anything structural, anything that adds square footage, or an ADU is more involved, and on these hillside lots grading or slope can come into play. We figure out the track — and the jurisdiction — before we quote, so there are no surprises mid-job.

## ADUs in Fletcher Hills

ADUs — accessory dwelling units, or granny flats — are a common reason Fletcher Hills homeowners call, usually for an aging parent, an adult child, or long-term rental income. California's statewide ADU laws apply across every jurisdiction here, which has made these far easier to build than they used to be, but the **local** layer — exact setbacks, size limits, and process — follows whichever city (or the County) your parcel sits in. That's the Fletcher Hills twist again: two neighbors a few streets apart can be under different local rules.

In practice, many Fletcher Hills lots can support a detached ADU or a garage conversion under the state framework, and the older detached garages common in the neighborhood sometimes convert well. The specifics — how big, how close to the property line, whether your lot and utilities support it, and whether the hillside brings grading into it — depend on your parcel and your jurisdiction. We run that feasibility check first, confirm the governing city or County, and only then move into design, so the finished ADU is legal, financeable, and something you'd actually want to live in.
