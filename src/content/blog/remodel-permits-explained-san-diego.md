The word "permit" makes a lot of people groan. It sounds like paperwork, fees, and waiting. But a permit is really just the city's way of checking that work in your home was done safely and correctly. In other words, it's there to protect *you*.

Let's clear up what permits are, when you need one, and why skipping them is a bigger risk than it looks.

## What a permit actually does

A building permit is permission from your local government to do certain work. Along with it comes an inspection: someone checks that the electrical, plumbing, or structure meets code — the safety rules everyone has to follow.

Think of it like a second set of eyes. If a wall is holding up part of your house, or a wire could start a fire, the inspection is there to catch it. When the work passes, you have proof it was done right. That proof matters later, especially when you sell.

## When you usually need a permit

Rules vary (more on that in a second), but in general you'll need a permit when the work affects safety or the structure of the home. That typically includes:

- **Moving or removing walls**, especially ones that hold weight.
- **New or moved plumbing** — relocating a sink, shower, or gas line.
- **New or changed electrical** — adding circuits, panels, or major wiring.
- **Additions and ADUs** — anytime you're adding living space.
- **Big structural changes** — windows cut into walls, new doorways, roof changes.

A larger [kitchen](/services/kitchens) or [bathroom](/services/bathrooms) remodel that moves plumbing or electrical usually needs one. An [ADU](/services/adus) almost always does.

## When you usually don't

Smaller, surface-level work often doesn't need a permit. Things like:

- Painting walls.
- Swapping cabinets or countertops in the same spot.
- Replacing a faucet or light fixture with the same type.
- New flooring over an existing floor.

The line isn't always obvious, though — which is exactly why a good contractor checks for you before the work starts, instead of guessing.

## The part people get wrong: the rules change by city

Here's the thing San Diego homeowners need to know. "San Diego" isn't one single set of rules. Your project might fall under the **City of San Diego**, or **El Cajon**, **La Mesa**, **Santee**, or the **unincorporated County** — and each one has its own process, its own fees, and its own timelines.

That means the cost and the wait for a permit in [La Mesa](/serving/la-mesa) can be different from the next city over. We're not going to throw out a specific dollar amount or number of days here, because it honestly depends on where you live and what you're building. The right move is to check your specific jurisdiction — and a contractor who works in your area will know how to do that.

If anyone promises you an exact permit cost or timeline without first knowing which city you're in, be a little skeptical. The honest answer is "it depends, and here's how we find out."

## Why you should never skip a permit to save money

Sometimes a cheap crew will offer to skip the permit to save you time and money. It sounds helpful. It's not. Here's what can go wrong:

- **Trouble when you sell.** Buyers and their lenders often ask for permit records. Unpermitted work can kill a deal or force you to fix it on the buyer's timeline.
- **Insurance headaches.** If something goes wrong — a fire, a leak — an insurer may push back on a claim tied to unpermitted work.
- **Redo costs.** If the city finds out, you may have to open walls back up to prove the work was done right. That costs far more than the permit would have.

Permits are cheap insurance. Skipping them is a gamble that usually doesn't pay.

Permits aren't red tape to fear. They're a safety check and a record that protects your home and your wallet. You need them for anything that touches safety or structure, you can skip them for small cosmetic work, and the exact rules depend on your city here in San Diego.
